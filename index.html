About CRHESI

The Centre for Research on Health Equity and Social Inclusion (CRHESI) is an interdisciplinary research initiative based at Western University's Faculty of Health Sciences. Established through a university-community partnership, CRHESI brings together researchers, students, service providers, policymakers, funders, and people with lived experience to advance health equity and social inclusion in London and surrounding communities.

Its work focuses on supporting community-engaged research, building trusted partnerships, and mobilizing evidence to inform policy, service delivery, and positive social change.

Background

Sociodemographic (SD) data collection is vital for informing healthcare and social service centres on how to best serve their communities. SD data provide insights into identifying gaps for underserved communities and supporting decision-making in resource allocation for these services. An Ontario mandate for SD data collection was ordered in 2020.

During the data collection process, CRHESI's community partners face challenges in collecting these data not only systematically, but also equitably and meaningfully. This project investigates those barriers and identifies best practices.

Problem Definition

When the project was first tasked to our team, the core challenge was ambiguous: isn't data collection straightforward? To define the problem clearly, we took three steps:

  • Internal discussion — My teammate and I discussed what the problem could really be in data collection practices, drawing on our backgrounds to hypothesize where barriers might exist.
  • Initial research — We used literature and anecdotal sources to verify whether the problems we identified were documented and real.
  • Validation with project host — We brought our findings back to CRHESI to ensure our understanding of project objectives and scope aligned with community partner needs.
Our primary objective: identify systematic barriers in achieving equitable and meaningful data collection practices, and surface existing best practices that address those barriers.

We focused on three guiding questions: What do poor data collection practices look like? How do they impact every stakeholder's experience? How might CRHESI provide guidance to work toward best practices for equitable change?

Research Methods

To answer these questions, we designed a two-method research approach drawing on five key informant groups.

Qualitative Interviews

We conducted one-on-one interviews with four informants — one from each key area of expertise. Each lasted 30–60 minutes, guided by a structured interview guide with flexibility to adapt. A laddering technique was used to probe motivations and underlying assumptions.

Literature Review

We reviewed 40+ articles using targeted search strategies across Scopus, PubMed, Google Scholar, and Western University's Libraries. Boolean operators and advanced filters ensured comprehensive coverage. This supplemented client perspectives where direct interviews were not feasible due to ethics timelines.

The five key informant groups were: administrative staff / data collectors, senior management, data governance experts, trauma- and violence-informed care (TVIC) experts, and clients (sourced through literature).

Outcomes & Completion Rates

Across CRHESI's learning collaborative, participating organizations showed measurable improvement in sociodemographic data completion rates between 2021/22 and 2023/24 — across all four indicator categories.

Figure 1: Average completion rates for sociodemographic indicators, CRHESI Learning Collaborative. 2021/22 vs. 2023/24. Disability showed the greatest improvement (+14.3%), with an overall increase of +8.1% across all indicators.

What We Learned

Administrative Staff / Data Collectors

Staff understand the value of data but may feel uncomfortable asking sensitive questions without clear scripts or training. Data collection works best when integrated naturally into workflow and clients clearly understand participation is voluntary.

Senior Management

Collecting data alone is not enough — organizations must have capacity to analyze, report, and act on it. Cross-organizational collaboration is critical for collective impact and shared learning.

Data Governance Experts

SD data requires clear standards for definition, storage, protection, and reporting. Inconsistent categories, unclear ownership, and poor data quality limit usefulness and can create risks for clients and communities.

TVIC Experts

Clients may feel unsafe or intruded upon without understanding why data is requested. Building trust first, using trauma- and violence-informed approaches, and ensuring clients can decline without consequence is essential.

Effective sociodemographic data collection is both a technical and relational process. Standardized tools and governance are necessary but not sufficient — success depends on staff confidence, leadership support, culturally safe practices, and genuine trust with clients.

Reflection

This project is ongoing. The synthesis of qualitative interviews and literature review is being developed into actionable guidance for CRHESI's community partners. The goal is not just better data — but data collection practices that are experienced as respectful, voluntary, and meaningfully connected to equity-focused outcomes for the communities they serve.
